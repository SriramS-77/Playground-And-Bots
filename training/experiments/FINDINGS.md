## 3b. How much did the train/eval overlap actually cost?

Notebook 03's size-matched control failed, and its own output shows why: **Static Multi-Threshold posted a seen-minus-unseen gap of +23.2 DI, and it never trains on anything.** Pool difficulty swamped the effect.

03b swaps roles instead. Two agents are trained on two halves; each half serves once as seen and once as unseen, so pool difficulty enters both sides of the difference and cancels by construction. For an untrained policy the two sets are literally the same runs, so its gap must be exactly zero -- which the notebook asserts as an implementation check.

| policy                  |   seen |   unseen |   gap |   se |   ci_lo |   ci_hi |
|:------------------------|-------:|---------:|------:|-----:|--------:|--------:|
| DQN                     |  47.41 |    40.34 |  7.08 | 1.63 |     3.9 |    10.3 |
| DQN without H-Score     |  17.72 |    17.71 |  0.01 | 0.11 |    -0.2 |     0.2 |
| Static Multi-Threshold  |  24.37 |    24.37 |  0    | 0    |     0   |     0   |
| Static Single-Threshold |  13.08 |    13.08 |  0    | 0    |     0   |     0   |

The mechanism is identified by the ablation. The no-Humanity-Score agent sees only `[captchas_solved, last_threat, n_active]` -- nothing that identifies a recording -- and shows **exactly zero** leakage. The full agent, whose state includes the behavioural score, is the one that leaks. So the overlap reached the agent through the scorer's outputs on recordings it had trained on, and nowhere else.

**This is the number the revision should report for R1.2**, not the drop from the published Table 4 in section 3.

| DQN (population frozen at 100) |   44.5 |   7   |
| DQN (population randomised)    |    1.3 |   1.5 |

Only the **frozen** variant is a valid test: it keeps the input inside the training distribution and removes just its information. The randomised variant also pushes the input out of distribution, so its collapse is ordinary OOD failure, not evidence of reliance. Freezing the population feature at a constant costs nothing, so the policy is **not** reading the attack level off total population.

## 7. A reward the system could actually have

A deployed controller knows neither the user's class nor `B_s` at decision time, and the immediate signal is ambiguous: *challenge failed, session ends* is a blocked bot **or** a false-positived human; *challenge passed, session continues* is a leaked bot **or** a satisfied human. The proxy reward is a pure function of the observable tuple, so both members of each pair get identical immediate reward. Class information reaches the agent only through a delayed, sparse, noisy abuse label.

| observation      |   n |   bot_blocked |   human_false_positive |   human_abandoned |   bot_leaked |   human_passed |
|:-----------------|----:|--------------:|-----------------------:|------------------:|-------------:|---------------:|
| failed_gone      | 203 |         0.951 |                  0.049 |                 0 |        0     |          0     |
| passed_left      |  33 |         0     |                  0     |                 1 |        0     |          0     |
| passed_continued | 899 |         0     |                  0     |                 0 |        0.174 |          0.826 |

| policy                  |     DI |   BOS |   SI_F1 |   false_positive_rate |
|:------------------------|-------:|------:|--------:|----------------------:|
| DQN                     | 43.797 | 0.64  |   0.631 |                 0.118 |
| DQN (oracle reward)     | 44.271 | 0.643 |   0.634 |                 0.158 |
| DQN (proxy reward)      | 40.743 | 0.538 |   0.782 |                 0     |
| Static Multi-Threshold  | 21.751 | 0.417 |   0.406 |                 0.122 |
| Static Single-Threshold | 22.487 | 0.394 |   0.389 |                 0.259 |

**The proxy-trained agent recovers 92% of the oracle-trained agent's average DI** (40.7 vs 44.3), judged entirely on ground-truth labels it never saw during training.

**Neither is the penalty weight.** Holding coverage at 0.3 and sweeping the abuse penalty (the arithmetic break-even is around 260):

|   abuse_penalty |    DI |   humans_left |   bots_left |   friction_s |
|----------------:|------:|--------------:|------------:|-------------:|

Only at -2400 does the agent defend at all, and it still leaks ~9x what the oracle-trained agent does. At -9600 it reverts to doing nothing, which is consistent with training instability at large reward magnitudes rather than with any property of the reward.

### The honest conclusion

A reward built only from observable signals produces a **usable** policy but not a **secure** one, and no configuration tested here closes the gap: label coverage 0.02-1.0, abuse penalty -150 to -9600, credit window 3 or 12 steps. The asymmetry is structural -- friction and abandonment are observed immediately and exactly on every session, abuse only rarely, late and noisily -- so an agent optimising the observable signal systematically under-weights security.

This is a limitation to publish with the evidence attached, not a solved substitution. It says Reviewer 1's deployment-reward objection is deeper than a reward-engineering exercise, which is worth more to the field than the positive result we set out to find.

## 9. Three participants: what the data can support

The 44 human recordings come from **3 participants**, across two collection sittings three months apart. Participant and setup are therefore confounded: the features that most separate the two campaigns are `pause_frac` and `dt_median`, which change with hardware and browser as readily as with the person.

| spec      |   dxdy - xy  (drop absolute position) |   dxdy_dt - dxdy  (add timing on top) |   xy_dt - xy  (add timing, keep position) |
|:----------|--------------------------------------:|--------------------------------------:|------------------------------------------:|
| lstm16    |                                -0.014 |                                 0.057 |                                     0.014 |
| lstm16_8  |                                 0.061 |                                -0     |                                     0.004 |
| lstm32    |                                 0.03  |                                 0.008 |                                    -0.02  |
| lstm32_16 |                                 0.048 |                                -0.023 |                                     0.002 |
| lstm64_32 |                                 0.078 |                                 0.006 |                                     0.005 |
| published |                                 0.021 |                                -0.007 |                                    -0.015 |

* `dxdy - xy  (drop absolute position)` — mean +0.037, positive in 5/6
* `dxdy_dt - dxdy  (add timing on top)` — mean +0.007, positive in 3/6
* `xy_dt - xy  (add timing, keep position)` — mean -0.002, positive in 4/6

Adding timing barely moves cross-validated performance and its sign flips between architectures. Worse, it **more than doubles the optimism gap** (0.087 -> 0.222): inter-event timing largely encodes the client's sampling rate, which is exactly what separates the two campaigns. The manuscript's acceleration/jerk language should be replaced with *the model reads relative motion, not absolute position or timing*.

**No augmentation wins.** This reverses what notebook 02 suggested, and the reason is instructive: per-movement jitter helped when the model was a 304k-parameter network reading *absolute* coordinates, because it damped a badly overparameterised fit. On a right-sized model reading *differences*, the same jitter corrupts the signal directly -- a 1px perturbation of each point is a large relative change to a step of a few pixels. Augmentation was compensating for a mis-specified model; fix the model and it is no longer needed.
