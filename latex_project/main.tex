\title{Balancing Security and Usability: Adaptive CAPTCHA Orchestration via Reinforcement Learning}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affil{Affiliation Omitted for Double-Anonymized Review}

\begin{abstract}
Risk-based bot detectors return a continuous score for each user rather than a decision, yet turning that score into an action (no challenge, a CAPTCHA of some difficulty, or a block) is still left to hand-written rules that trade security against usability. We study whether reinforcement learning (RL) can learn this mapping over an 11-level intervention ladder. 
A compact LSTM \emph{Humanity Scorer} reads mouse dynamics (displacement, inter-event time, speed and acceleration) and outputs a calibrated bot probability every 10\,s. 
With a running session score, challenge history and the number of active users, it forms the state of a DQN, PPO, Neural-LinUCB or Neural Thompson Sampling controller. Controllers are trained and evaluated in a stochastic simulator in which bots solve challenges with an item-response model and human pass rates and solve times come from published CAPTCHA user studies, so usability is \emph{modelled, not measured}. 
On held-out recordings, PPO and DQN reach a Discrimination Index (humans kept \% $-$ bots through \%) of 99.8 and 97.8 ($\pm$0.6 and $\pm$1.2, 95\% CI over 5 seeds) against 95.5 for the best static threshold policy. 
When covert bots shift the scorer's calibration, frozen RL policies degrade faster than static thresholds. 
Training PPO online only once a label-free change detector fires keeps 99.8 without drift, alarms within 1--5 episodes of the attack, and reaches 85.1 and 82.0 at half and full shift, matching or exceeding online training with labels (83.3 and 82.6) and far above the best static policy (39.5 at full shift). RL can thus replace hand-tuned thresholds if the score is calibrated and monitored for drift; attacks that destroy the score's ranking remain out of reach.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{utomated} traffic is now over half of all web requests, and 37\% of 2024 traffic was attributed to malicious bots \cite{imperva2025}. Bots scrape content, post spam \cite{ferrara2016} and stuff credentials \cite{ba2021}. The standard defence is the CAPTCHA \cite{von_2003,guerar2021}, but every challenge also costs legitimate users time, and some fail or leave: in a large-scale study of 318{,}000 CAPTCHAs, humans solved distorted-text schemes correctly only 70--98\% of the time \cite{bursztein_humans_2010}, and a recent study of modern CAPTCHAs found that a substantial share of participants abandoned a task when a challenge appeared \cite{captcha_study_2023}. Meanwhile, vision-language models solve many visual challenges \cite{qi2026,wu2025mca_bench}, and reCAPTCHA v2 has been broken with fine-tuned object detectors \cite{plesner2024yolo}.

Risk-based services such as reCAPTCHA v3 therefore return a continuous score rather than a verdict \cite{reCAPTCHA}, which moves the hard decision to the website operator: what should happen to a user whose score is 0.6? Three properties make this decision difficult. \emph{One threshold does not fit every context}: a score that justifies frictionless browsing may be inadequate for a payment page \cite{sepczuk_2018}, and fixed thresholds become stale as adversaries adapt \cite{sethi_2018,sivakorn_recaptcha_2016}. \emph{Scores are not comparable across providers}: each detector has its own undisclosed calibration, so combining them becomes ad-hoc \cite{yousef_2023}. \emph{The trade-off is sequential}: a mild first challenge can be escalated if a user still looks suspicious, and a policy that challenges early can relax later. Rule-based web application firewalls \cite{cloudArmour} encode these choices by hand, and the CAPTCHA schemes they trigger are each a single challenge type \cite{umar_2022} rather than a graded response.

We ask whether an RL controller can learn the score-to-action mapping, and what a simulation-based evaluation can and cannot establish about it. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*), leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{A graduated orchestration formulation.} Every 10\,s of a session the controller chooses one of 11 levels (0: no challenge; 1--9: increasingly demanding challenges; 10: strongest fallback), from a behavioural score and session and server context (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A compact, calibrated behavioural scorer} (6{,}081 parameters), which matches or exceeds the mouse-movement detector of Iliou et al.\ \cite{iliou_2021} on their benchmark after fine-tuning (Sections~\ref{sec:method}-\ref{sec:scorer} and \ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:res_scorer}).
\item \textbf{A literature-grounded simulator}: bots solve challenges with a capability-dependent probability, and human pass rates and solve times come from published user studies \cite{bursztein_humans_2010,captcha_study_2023} (Section~\ref{sec:method}-\ref{sec:env}).
\item \textbf{An evaluation with confidence intervals} of DQN, PPO, Neural-LinUCB and Neural Thompson Sampling against static thresholds and a no-score ablation, on held-out recordings, under varying human and bot volumes (Section~\ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:res_main}).
\item \textbf{Results that bound deployment}: rewards that need no labels, and frozen offline versus online training comparison when the scorer's calibration drifts (Section~\ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:drift}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{RELATED WORK}
\subsection{CAPTCHA Security and Usability}
CAPTCHAs were introduced as hard AI problems \cite{von_2003} and have been surveyed extensively \cite{guerar2021,umar_2022}. Yan and El~Ahmad analysed usability factors in CAPTCHA design \cite{yan2008captcha}; Bursztein et al.\ measured human accuracy and solve time on 21 schemes \cite{bursztein_humans_2010}; and Searles et al.\ measured solve times, preferences and abandonment for modern challenges with 1{,}400 participants \cite{captcha_study_2023}. On the attack side, text CAPTCHAs have generic solvers \cite{bursztein2014new}, reCAPTCHA has been bypassed with browser cookies and history \cite{sivakorn_recaptcha_2016} and with image models \cite{plesner2024yolo}, its v3 score has been gamed with RL \cite{akrout2019}, and multimodal models solve visual-reasoning challenges \cite{qi2026,wu2025mca_bench}.

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Behavioural Biometrics}
Mouse dynamics distinguish humans from bots \cite{bezier_bots_2022,iliou_2021} and individuals from each other \cite{khan_2023,shen_2013}. Human motion follows Fitts' law with motor noise \cite{fittslaw2019}, while bots range from straight-line movers to B\'ezier-curve and learned trajectory generators \cite{bezier2025,bezier_bots_2022,antal_2021_sapi}. Iliou et al.\ detected advanced web bots by fusing a web-log classifier with a mouse-movement classifier \cite{iliou_2021}; Acien et al.\ used touch and sensor data \cite{acien_2021}. Recurrent networks such as the LSTM \cite{hochreiter_lstm_1997} have been used to detect bots from mouse-movement sequences \cite{he_seq_2021}.

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Adaptive Challenges and RL}
Aura-CAPTCHA uses RL and GANs to adapt challenge difficulty \cite{aura_captcha_2025} but does not orchestrate a graded response ladder. Contextual bandits choose an action from features to maximise immediate reward \cite{li_linucb_2010,russo_ts_2018}, while DQN \cite{mnih_dqn_2015} and PPO \cite{schulman_ppo_2017} optimise discounted return in a Markov decision process \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. RL approaches have been applied to fraud and anomaly detection \cite{cui_2025}. Labelled security data, including human/bot interaction data, remain scarce \cite{mvula_2023}, which is why we report data sizes, splits and leakage controls in detail.

\section{METHODOLOGY}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{System Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system_architecture} shows the four tiers. \emph{Tier 1} (client telemetry) collects cursor coordinates and timestamps in the browser and converts them into a platform-independent feature. \emph{Tier 2} (Humanity Scorer) converts each 10\,s chunk of telemetry into a bot probability; any scorer with a probabilistic output, including a commercial one, can be substituted. \emph{Tier 3} (RL controller) maps the score and context to a level. \emph{Tier 4} (fulfilment) routes each level to a concrete challenge; in this paper the outcome of each level is simulated (Section~\ref{sec:method}-\ref{sec:env}).

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}
\subsubsection{Our Recordings}
A playground blog website (navigation, reading, form filling) recorded \texttt{mousemove} events $(x, y, t)$ with a minimum gap of 10\,ms; each session lasted about 120\,s.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Bot Hierarchy}
We implemented four levels of automated agents that perform the same tasks on the same site:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Naive bots} perform only the task (clicking a button, filling a form) with no attempt to look human; the cursor teleports directly to each target.
\item \emph{Human-like bots} move the cursor along straight lines instead of teleporting, but at constant speed, without realistic acceleration.
\item \emph{Mimic bots} follow non-straight B\'ezier curves with varying speed to emulate human motor characteristics \cite{bezier2025,bezier_bots_2022}.
\item \emph{Fallible bots}, the most advanced, add human-like imperfections: occasional errors, hesitations and corrections such as overshooting a target and adjusting.
\end{itemize}
Human-like mouse movement lets advanced bots evade movement-based bot detectors \cite{bezier_bots_2022}, which is why the upper tiers imitate human motion rather than only completing the task. The dataset has 156 recordings (44 human, 112 bot; 42{,}913 movements); the human recordings come from 3 participants. The study followed established ethical guidelines for research with human participants: participants gave informed consent, and only anonymous cursor trajectories were stored, with no personally identifiable information retained.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Partition}
Recordings are first split into a scorer block, an RL block and a held-out \emph{evaluation block}. The evaluation block has 14 human and 36 bot recordings and is never used to fit or select any model, avoiding data snooping \cite{arp_2022}. The scorer block alone (54 recordings) is too little data to train the scorer stably, so the scorer and RL blocks are combined into one training pool, and cross-fitting \cite{wolpert_1992,chernozhukov_2018} lets this pool serve both the scorer and the RL policy without leakage. The pool is split by recording into five parts; five scorers are each trained on four parts and score the RL-block recordings of the remaining part. The policy is trained on these out-of-fold scores, so no RL training session is scored by a model that saw it; otherwise the policy would learn from scores that are more confident than any it meets in deployment. For evaluation, one scorer is refitted on the whole pool and scores only the evaluation block.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Web Bot Detection Dataset}
The web bot detection dataset of Iliou et al.\ \cite{iliou_2021} contains browser sessions on a Wikipedia-mirror site, with mouse movements and web logs, from humans, \emph{moderate} bots (browser fingerprint and scripted movement) and \emph{advanced} bots (browser fingerprint and human-like movement). Phase~1 has 50 human, 50 moderate and 50 advanced sessions and two benchmarks with fixed session-level splits: D1 (humans + moderate bots) and D2 (humans + advanced bots), each with 35+35 training and 15+15 test sessions. Phase~2 has sessions from 28 other users and new, separate bot sessions. We use Phase~1's moderate and advanced bots, through D1 and D2, for the benchmark comparison and fine-tuning (Section~\ref{sec:method}-\ref{sec:scorer}), and only Phase~2's advanced bots, as covert bots, in the drift study (Section~\ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:drift}). Phase~2's moderate bots were excluded after a local check: our scorer failed against them and their ranking overlaps with humans, whereas the calibration-shift study needs bots whose scores move but whose ranking above humans survives. This was an accepted flaw of the Humanity Scorer in this experiment, since training data was not diverse enough to adapt to unseen data without finetuning.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Balabit Mouse Dynamics Challenge}
The Balabit Mouse Dynamics Challenge \cite{balabit_2016} contains remote-desktop mouse sessions of 10 users. It contains only humans, so we use it only as a population of unseen humans.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Preparation of External Data}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Cleaning (all external data):} Events with pixel coordinates outside $[0,10^4]$ are dropped, and so is any event less than 10\,ms after the previous kept one, resampling every source to our logger's rate.
\item \emph{Benchmark (Phase~1 D1/D2):} Each session is cut into consecutive 10\,s chunks; chunks with fewer than two events are dropped. Sessions and splits are the dataset's own.
\item \emph{Drift study (Phase~2 advanced bots, Balabit):} Only the first 120\,s of a session is kept, and a session needs at least 30\,s and 20 events to qualify. This leaves 120 advanced-bot sessions. Balabit is capped at 30 qualifying sessions (drawn with fixed random seed) per user, so that no user dominates: 300 sessions from 10 users.
\item \emph{Splitting for online training and evaluation:} Each population is split once: the advanced-bot sessions into two halves of 60 by a fixed-seed shuffle, and Balabit by user, 5 users (150 sessions) per half, so evaluation humans are people never seen in training. One half supplies the covert bots or bot-like humans mixed into online training traffic, the other only into evaluation traffic.
\end{itemize}


\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Humanity Scorer}
\label{sec:scorer}
\subsubsection{Input}
A chunk is converted to per-event features $[\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta t, v, a]$: displacement, inter-event time, speed $v=\lVert(\Delta x,\Delta y)\rVert/\Delta t$ and acceleration $a=\Delta v/\Delta t$, both finite-difference estimates between consecutive events. Absolute screen position is not used, because it encodes page layout rather than behaviour. Features are standardised with training statistics and cut into windows of 32 events; the last window is padded and a sixth binary channel marks padded steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:lstm}).

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Model}
One LSTM layer \cite{hochreiter_lstm_1997} with 32 units reads the window. Given the input $x_t$, previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ and cell state $c_{t-1}$, it computes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
f_t &= \sigma(W_f x_t + U_f h_{t-1} + b_f),\\
i_t &= \sigma(W_i x_t + U_i h_{t-1} + b_i),\\
o_t &= \sigma(W_o x_t + U_o h_{t-1} + b_o),\\
\tilde c_t &= \tanh(W_c x_t + U_c h_{t-1} + b_c),\\
c_t &= f_t\odot c_{t-1} + i_t\odot\tilde c_t,\qquad h_t = o_t\odot\tanh(c_t),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the forget, input and output gates $f_t, i_t, o_t$ decide what the cell keeps, adds and exposes, and $\odot$ is the element-wise product. The final hidden state $h_{32}$ passes through a 32-unit ReLU layer and a single linear output, the logit $z$ (6{,}081 parameters in total). Training uses binary cross-entropy with class-balanced weights, Adam (learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$), dropout 0.25 and recurrent dropout 0.2, with early stopping on a validation split of recordings.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Temperature Calibration}
Since the RL state and the rewards treat the score as a probability, the network's output is calibrated after training by temperature scaling \cite{guo_calibration_2017}. For a window with logit $z$, the calibrated probability is $\sigma(z/T)$, where the single parameter $T>0$ is chosen to minimise the log-loss on the validation split and the network weights are left unchanged; $T>1$ softens over-confident outputs and $T<1$ sharpens under-confident ones, while the ranking of windows, and hence the AUC, is unaffected. The scorer used throughout has $T=1.14$. The chunk score is the mean of its windows' calibrated probabilities, $q=P(\text{bot})$, and the Humanity Score is $H=1-q$. Section~\ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:res_scorer} reports its calibration on held-out recordings.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Fine-Tuning on the Web Bot Benchmark}
We use the benchmark's own split of D1 and D2 into 70 training sessions (35 human, 35 bot) and 30 test sessions (15 and 15). Each session is cut into chunks as in Section~\ref{sec:method}-\ref{sec:data}, using all its \texttt{mousemove} events (no 120\,s limit). Each chunk becomes 32-event windows of $[\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta t,v,a]$ plus the padding mask, standardised with the pre-trained scorer's statistics, as in Section~\ref{sec:method}-\ref{sec:scorer}. This gives 1{,}681 training and 719 test chunks for D1 and 2{,}186 and 942 for D2. The model is our final refitted scorer fine-tuned on the training sessions: 20\% of the training sessions of each class (7 human and 7 bot) are held out for early stopping (patience 10, till 100 epochs) and for calibrating the temperature, while the rest are used for training with Adam (learning rate $10^{-4}$), batch size 64 and class-balanced weights. A chunk's score is the mean of its windows' calibrated probabilities, and a session's score is the mean of its chunk scores, thresholded at 0.5. We repeat this with five seeds, which change the validation split and the randomness of training (batch order and dropout), and report the mean.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Design Rationale}
The architecture and input representation were selected by 5-fold cross-validation grouped by session, over LSTM sizes from 8 to 64 units, one or two layers, and inputs ranging from raw coordinates to the kinematic features above. Small models generalised best to unseen data splits. Data augmentation (Gaussian or per-movement jitter) did not improve this model and hence is not used.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Static Baselines}
Four untrained policies map the score straight to a level. The \emph{static threshold} shows level 10 when $q>0.5$, and the \emph{multi-threshold} shows level $\lfloor10q\rfloor$. The \emph{NP threshold} sets $t_\alpha$ by the Neyman--Pearson criterion \cite{scott_nowak_2005,tong_np_2018}, so that at most a fraction $\alpha$ of the scorer block's human sessions would ever be challenged, and shows level 10 above it; the \emph{NP multi-threshold} maps ten equal bands of $(t_\alpha,1]$ to levels 1--10. We use $\alpha=0.1$ ($t=0.685$), the best of the values tried in Section~\ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:drift}.

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{RL Formulation}
\label{sec:rl}
Each user session is an episode of up to 12 decision steps, one per 10\,s chunk. Many users are active at once, and the controller acts for each active user at each step.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{State}
Five features in $[0,1]$: (1) the current chunk score $q_t$; (2) the running mean of $q$ over the session; (3) the number of challenges the user has passed, $\tanh(n/5)$; (4) the last level shown, divided by 10; and (5) the number of active users, scaled. Features (3)--(5) need no scorer.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Actions}
Level $T\in\{0,\dots,10\}$. Level 0 shows nothing; levels 1--2 are invisible to the user (passive score, honeypot); levels 3--9 are visible challenges of increasing difficulty; level 10 is the strongest level, an audio fallback that humans pass about half the time and only the most capable bots solve; it is not a hard block, so no level removes a user with certainty (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}).

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Reward (Training Only)}
For a bot of capability $B_s$: if it is stopped, $r = R_{\max} - c_o\max(0, T-B_s)$, which discourages stronger levels than needed; if it passes, $r = R_{\text{leak}} - c_u\max(0, B_s-T)$. For a human: $r=R_{\max}$ at $T=0$; $r = R_{\max}/2 - c_f\,s_T$ after passing a challenge that took $s_T$ seconds; and $r = R_{\text{FP}} - c_f\,s_T$ if the human fails it (a false positive). Friction is priced per measured second, so the human cost of a level follows the solve times of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}. This reward needs the user's true class, which the simulator knows and a deployed system does not.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Worked Example}
A fallible bot of capability $B_s=4$ produces a second chunk with $q=0.82$; its session mean is 0.75, it has passed no challenges, its last level was 0 and 400 users are active. The state is $(0.82, 0.75, 0, 0, 0.67)$. If the policy chooses $T=6$ (image grid), the bot passes with probability $\sigma(1.5(4.5-6))=0.095$. If it is stopped, the session ends and $r=50-2.5\cdot2=45$; if it passes, $r=-150$ and its next state records one passed challenge and last level 0.6. A human shown the same level passes with probability 0.86 in a median 9.7\,s, giving $r=25-2\cdot9.7=5.6$, or fails, giving $r=-100-19.4$. A trained DQN typically shows nothing to a user with $q<0.2$ and escalates through levels 3--7 as $q$ and the running mean rise.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Online Rewards}
The reward above needs the class. We also train with four rewards that need at most a small labelled sample. Let $o_t$ be the observable outcome of step $t$ (no challenge, passed, failed and continued, failed and left), $\bar r_H(T,o)$ the human reward at expected friction, and $r_B(b,T,o)$ the reward of a bot of capability $b$, with prior $\varrho(b)$ uniform over $0,\dots,9$. The \emph{score-only} reward reads the chunk score as $P(\text{bot})$:
\begin{equation}
\hat r^{\,\text{score}}_t=(1-q_t)\,\bar r_H(T_t,o_t)+q_t\textstyle\sum_b\varrho(b)\,r_B(b,T_t,o_t).
\label{eq:scoreonly}
\end{equation}
The \emph{posterior} reward replaces $q_t$ by the posterior over class and capability given the score and the session's outcomes so far:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
w_t(H)&\propto(1-\hat\pi)\textstyle\prod_{j\le t}P_H(o_j\mid T_j),\\
w_t(B,b)&\propto\hat\pi\,\Lambda_t\,\varrho(b)\textstyle\prod_{j\le t}P_B(o_j\mid T_j,b),\\
\hat r^{\,\text{post}}_t&=w_t(H)\,\bar r_H(T_t,o_t)\\
&\quad+\textstyle\sum_b w_t(B,b)\,r_B(b,T_t,o_t),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
normalised to sum to one. $P_H$, $P_B$ are the simulator's outcome probabilities, so this reward is an upper bound for the idea; $\Lambda_t$ is the score likelihood ratio from a Platt calibration \cite{platt_1999} fitted on the scorer block; and $\hat\pi$ is the episode's bot share, estimated by EM from the users' first-step ratios \cite{saerens_2002}.

The two \emph{audit} rewards recalibrate the score on current traffic from a random 2\% of users per episode. With labels (from review or confirmed abuse), ten score bins $k(q)=\min(\lfloor10q\rfloor,9)$ with centres $c_k$ keep decayed counts of audited steps and bots, a variant of histogram-binning recalibration \cite{zadrozny_2001}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
n_k&\leftarrow0.97\,n_k+|\mathcal A_{e,k}|,\\
m_k&\leftarrow0.97\,m_k+\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal A_{e,k}}y_i,\\
\hat p_k&=\frac{m_k+2c_k}{n_k+2},\qquad \hat r^{\,\text{audit}}_t=\hat r^{\,\text{score}}_t\big|_{q_t\to\hat p_{k(q_t)}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:recal}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal A_{e,k}$ holds the audited steps of episode $e$ in bin $k$ and $y_i=1$ for a bot; audited users keep the label-based reward. With a level-9 challenge as the audit, shown before the policy acts and kept on at evaluation, only pass or fail is observed: $m_k$ counts failures, and the bot share follows from the known fail rates $f_H=0.144$, $f_B=0.887$ by a smoothed adjusted classify-and-count \cite{forman_2008},
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat f_k&=\frac{m_k+2\,[c_kf_B+(1-c_k)f_H]}{n_k+2},\\
\hat p_k&=\mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]}\Big(\frac{\hat f_k-f_H}{f_B-f_H}\Big),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:recal_ch}
\end{equation}
which then replaces $q_t$ in (\ref{eq:scoreonly}) for every user.

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Learning Algorithms}
\label{sec:algos}
\subsubsection{DQN}
DQN \cite{mnih_dqn_2015} approximates the optimal action-value function $Q^*(s,a)$ with a 128--64 ReLU network $Q(s,a;\theta)$ that outputs one value per level. Transitions $(s,a,r,s')$ are stored in a replay memory $\mathcal D$, and the network minimises the temporal-difference loss
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\theta)=\mathbb E_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\ell_\delta\big(&r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta^-)\\
&-Q(s,a;\theta)\big)\Big],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_\delta$ is the Huber loss and $\theta^-$ are the parameters of a target network copied from $\theta$ every 200 steps. Actions are chosen $\epsilon$-greedily: a random level with probability $\epsilon$ (decayed from 1.0 to 0.01), otherwise $\arg\max_a Q(s,a;\theta)$. The replay memory is split into a human and a bot buffer (10{,}000 transitions each), and every mini-batch draws half from each; this balancing uses the true class, as the reward does.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{PPO}
PPO \cite{schulman_ppo_2017} learns a stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ (11 logits) and a value function from the same 128--64 body. It maximises the clipped surrogate objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\big[\min\big(\kappa_t\hat A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(\kappa_t,1-\epsilon_c,1+\epsilon_c)\hat A_t\big)\big],
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_t=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)$ is the probability ratio, $\hat A_t$ the generalised advantage estimate and $\epsilon_c=0.2$; clipping stops a single update from moving the policy too far. Value-function and entropy terms are added with weights 0.5 and 0.01, and the policy is updated once per simulated episode.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Neural-LinUCB and Neural Thompson Sampling}
Neural-LinUCB and Neural Thompson Sampling \cite{li_linucb_2010,russo_ts_2018,riquelme_2018} treat each decision as a contextual bandit. An MLP maps the state to an embedding $\phi(s)$, and the expected reward of level $a$ is modelled as linear in it, $\mathbb E[r\mid s,a]=\phi(s)^\top\theta_a$. For each level the bandit keeps the ridge statistics
\begin{equation}
A_a=\lambda I+\sum_{i:a_i=a}\phi(s_i)\phi(s_i)^\top,\qquad b_a=\sum_{i:a_i=a}r_i\,\phi(s_i),
\end{equation}
so that $\hat\theta_a=A_a^{-1}b_a$ is the ridge estimate ($\lambda=1$), and the network weights are trained to reduce the squared error between observed and predicted rewards. LinUCB is optimistic under uncertainty: it picks
\begin{equation}
a_t=\arg\max_a\ \phi(s_t)^\top\hat\theta_a+\alpha\sqrt{\phi(s_t)^\top A_a^{-1}\phi(s_t)},\quad \alpha=1.
\end{equation}
Thompson Sampling instead keeps a posterior over each $\theta_a$, draws $\tilde\theta_a$ from it and picks $\arg\max_a\phi(s_t)^\top\tilde\theta_a$. With one component the sample is $\tilde\theta_a\sim\mathcal N(\hat\theta_a,\alpha^2A_a^{-1})$, with the covariance either diagonal or full. A single linear model per level cannot express that the same level pays very differently for a human and for a bot, so we also allowed a mixture of $K$ components per level, in the spirit of mixture-prior Thompson Sampling \cite{hong_mixts_2022}, fitted without labels on (embedding, reward) pairs: each component $k$ has its own ridge statistics and a Gaussian density over the embedding, and a context-dependent gate $\pi_k(\phi)\propto\pi_k\,\mathcal N(\phi;m_k,v_k)$ weights the components' sampled scores, $\text{score}(a)=\sum_k\pi_k(\phi)\,\phi^\top\tilde\theta_{a,k}$. With $K=1$ this reduces exactly to Gaussian Thompson Sampling. 

Four posteriors were compared in the architecture search: a single Gaussian with diagonal covariance, a single Gaussian with full covariance, and mixtures of two and three components (MoG2, MoG3), each combined with five embedding networks. 
This gives 20 candidates (4 posteriors $\times$ 5 networks).
% ; for each network and seed one bandit is trained and the four posteriors are refitted on its stored transitions. 
Each candidate was trained with 5 seeds and scored by 3-fold cross-validation on the RL block. 
LinUCB, whose rule has no posterior to choose, was searched over the same five embedding networks with its standard single-Gaussian statistics. 
Since $\phi$ changes whenever the network is updated, statistics accumulated under earlier embeddings become inconsistent; at the end of training we therefore recompute every $A_a$ and $b_a$ from the stored transitions in the final embedding space.

Fig.~\ref{fig:rl_diagrams} contrasts the two families: bandits treat each decision independently, while DQN and PPO propagate value across the steps of a session. Table~\ref{tab:config} lists the final configuration of every component.

\subsection{Simulated Environment}
\label{sec:env}
\subsubsection{Traffic}
Each training episode draws 50--250 humans uniformly and, independently, one bot volume from $\{0, 20, 100, 200, 500, 1000\}$, so the number of active users does not reveal the number of bots.
Each simulated user replays the mouse data of a session recording from the RL block, and each bot draws a capability $B_s$ uniformly from $\{0,\dots,9\}$. 
The trained policies are then evaluated, frozen, in two traffic settings, fixed and varying (Section~\ref{sec:method}-\ref{sec:metrics}).

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Challenge Outcomes}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} lists the 11 threat levels. 

\emph{Bots:} A bot's capability $B_s\in\{0,\dots,9\}$ sets the hardest level it solves reliably ($B_s-1$, pass probability 0.90); at level $B_s$ itself it passes about two times in three. 
It passes level $T\ge1$ with the two-parameter logistic item-response (2PL) probability $\sigma(\alpha(B_s+0.5-T))$, the standard model for an agent of latent ability attempting an item of given difficulty \cite{birnbaum_1968,embretson_irt_2000}, with discrimination $\alpha=1.5$, a value in the ``high'' range (1.35--1.69) of the usual classification of item discriminations \cite{baker_irt_2001}, chosen because each level of the ladder is meant to separate bot capabilities sharply while keeping outcomes probabilistic. 
No measurement fixes $\alpha$, so we treat it as a design choice and test its sensitivity over $\alpha\in\{0.5,1,1.5,3,6,12\}$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:sens}); level 0 shows nothing and is always passed. 
% The probability depends only on the gap $g=B_s-T$, so Table~\ref{tab:ladder}'s three columns determine every capability: a bot passes with probability $\ge$0.98 when $g\ge2$, 0.90 at $g=1$, 0.68 at $g=0$, 0.32 at $g=-1$, 0.095 at $g=-2$, 0.023 at $g=-3$ and $\le$0.005 when $g\le-4$. 
Strong bots occasionally fail easy challenges and weak bots occasionally pass hard ones. 
No published study reports solve rates for a common set of bots across all eleven mechanisms, so the per-level bot rates are model outputs rather than measurements; the ordering of the levels follows the attack literature in Table~\ref{tab:ladder} (passive scores and checkboxes are defeated by browser-driving bots, distorted text by generic solvers, image grids by object detectors, while reasoning games are solved only partially by recent multimodal agents). 

\emph{Humans:} A human passes each attempt with the measured pass probability of that challenge type and takes its median solve time; a human who fails may retry once and is lost after a second failure.
Even if capable of passing, a human leaves the site with probability $1-e^{-s_T/\tau}$, $\tau=45$\,s, where $s_T$ is the time spent.
This models human abandonment from friction.
We found no study that measures such leaving, so this term is a modelling assumption and we report its sensitivity (Section~\ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:sens}).

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
Each policy is trained on the RL block and evaluated, frozen, on simulated traffic built from the evaluation block in two settings: \emph{fixed} traffic of 100 humans with 20, 100, 200, 500 or 1000 bots, and \emph{varying} traffic in which the number of humans is drawn from 50--250 and bots make up 5--75\% of all users (so 3--750 bots). Each setting is run with 50 evaluation seeds for each of 5 training seeds.

Let $H_{\text{tot}}$ and $B_{\text{tot}}$ be the numbers of humans and bots in a run, and $H_{\text{surv}}$ and $B_{\text{surv}}$ the numbers that complete their session. With human survival $S_H=H_{\text{surv}}/H_{\text{tot}}$, bot survival $S_B=B_{\text{surv}}/B_{\text{tot}}$ and bot kill rate $K_B=1-S_B$:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Discrimination Index}, $\text{DI}=100(S_H-S_B)$: the margin between the share of humans kept and bots let through. It is our primary metric.
\item \textbf{Balanced Orchestration Score}, $\text{BOS}=2S_HK_B/(S_H+K_B)$: the harmonic mean of humans kept and bots stopped, which is low whenever either fails \cite{fscore_2023}.
\item \textbf{System-Purity F1}, $\text{SP-F1}=2PS_H/(P+S_H)$ with $P=H_{\text{surv}}/(H_{\text{surv}}+B_{\text{surv}})$: the purity of the traffic that gets through; it depends on the bot-to-human ratio.
\item \textbf{Scale-Invariant F1}, $\text{SI-F1}=2P_nS_H/(P_n+S_H)$ with $P_n=S_H/(S_H+S_B)$: a version of SP-F1 that weights 1\% of humans equally with 1\% of bots, so it does not depend on traffic volume.
\end{itemize}
We also report mean friction (seconds of challenge time per human). 
Bot survival is undefined without bots, so every run has at least one bot.
Uncertainty is reported across training seeds (each seed averaged over its evaluation runs) as a 95\% $t$-interval.

Removing the Humanity Score reduces DQN to DI 16.8, letting 45\% of bots through. 

The bandits are less stable: Thompson Sampling reaches DI 92.1 (worst seed 81.7) and LinUCB 64.7 (worst seed 44.9). 
Refitted on held-out recordings, the Thompson posteriors reproduce the ordering of the architecture search (single Gaussian 77.6 diagonal and 78.1 full, two components 87.1, three 92.1), so the gain comes from the number of components, not the covariance. 
Thompson Sampling (MoG3) with 3 components performs the best among them.
Optimising only the immediate reward, the bandits cannot plan an escalation over the session as DQN and PPO do. 
Under varying traffic every policy scores the same or slightly higher (PPO 99.96, DQN 99.4).
The evaluation block is small and well separated by the scorer, so these figures are close to a ceiling; Section~\ref{sec:results}-\ref{sec:drift} tests the policies when the score is less reliable.

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Sensitivity to Simulator Assumptions}
\label{sec:sens}
\subsubsection{Simulator Constants}
We re-evaluated the trained policies (without retraining) under bot-solve slopes $\alpha\in\{0.5,1,1.5,3,6,12\}$ and leave-after-pass constants $\tau\in\{22,45,80\}$\,s or no leaving. 
RL policies are stable across $\alpha$, while the multi-threshold policy drops to 69.6 at $\alpha=12$. 
Human abandonment matters most for policies that challenge humans often: as $\tau$ grows from 22\,s to no leaving, the multi-threshold policy rises from 66.2 to 97.9 and overtakes the static threshold (87.8 to 96.8).
PPO and DQN remain the best policies at every $\tau$.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Reward Constants}
DQN was retrained (5 seeds each) under nine alternative reward settings: leak penalty $R_{\text{leak}}\in\{-50,-300\}$, false-positive penalty $R_{\text{FP}}\in\{-50,-200\}$, friction $c_f\in\{1,4\}$ per second, overkill $c_o=1$ and underestimation $c_u\in\{2.5,10\}$. Eight settings give DI 97.5--98.7 (default 97.7), with no bot getting through in any of them. The exception is the mildest false-positive penalty ($R_{\text{FP}}=-50$): losing a human then costs little more than a challenge, and one of its five seeds kept only 70\% of humans (DI 70.4; mean 92.1). Every setting stays above the static threshold (90.3 on the same evaluation seeds) on average.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Population Input}
Freezing DQN's active-user feature at 25, 100 or 400 regardless of the real traffic leaves bot survival at zero (DI 100.0, against 96.6 unfrozen on the same grid of 25--400 humans and 20--1000 bots). Freezing it at 1400, above anything seen in training, makes the policy challenge more and keep fewer humans (87\%). The population count therefore controls how cautious the policy is, not whether it stops bots; detection comes from the score.

In our simulation 47.5\% of ``passed and continued'' events are bots and 11.6\% of ``failed and ended'' events are humans. We trained DQN with 12 rewards computed from such outcomes (different penalties, potential-based shaping, a learned reward model, delayed abuse labels): \emph{all 12 converged to never challenging anyone} (DI $\approx0$). \vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Covert Bots: Offline vs Online Training}
\label{sec:drift}
New bot types are the main threat a deployed scorer faces: bots that imitate human movement more closely than anything in the scorer's training data. We test two versions of this threat. 
In the first, the scorer's \emph{calibration} breaks while its ranking survives; in the second, the attacker destroys the ranking as well.

\vspace{-20pt}
\subsubsection{Covert Bots That Shift Calibration}
A fraction $\rho$ of the evaluation bots is replaced by Phase~2 advanced web bots \cite{iliou_2021}, whose movement imitates humans; training uses the other half of that population. 
Without fine-tuning, our scorer gives these bots a mean $P(\text{bot})$ of only 0.39, so as $\rho$ goes from 0 to 1 the bots' calibration error grows from 0.07 to 0.61, while session-level AUC stays $\ge0.99$: the bots still score above the humans, but far below what the policies learned to expect. 
We compare policies trained offline and evaluated frozen with DQN and PPO trained online for 300 episodes, with the covert bots present from episode 100, against the same protocol without them (control). 
This online training result for PPO under drift is shown in Table~\ref{tab:recal}.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Training Online Only When a Shift Is Detected}
Online training is needed under covert bots but costs performance when nothing has changed (PPO: 92.4 always online against 99.8 frozen without drift).

We compare the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance to the burn-in scores with the share of users in the middle band $[0.25,0.6]$, where neither humans (near 0.1) nor known bots (above 0.8) fall but covert bots do.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Covert Bots That Destroy the Ranking}
In a stronger attack, a fraction $\rho$ of bots \emph{replay} recorded human mouse trajectories from the evaluation block, so that at $\rho=1$ their score distribution equals the humans' (session AUC 0.75 at $\rho=0.5$, 0.50 at $\rho=1$).
Every policy, frozen or trained online, static or learned, then degrades linearly to DI $\approx0$ at $\rho=1$ (PPO 0.2, DQN $-0.1$, best static 3.8); even online training with all labels reaches only 22 DI for DQN, by challenging every user.
This is a limit of the information available, not of the learner: when the attacker reproduces the very features the detector uses, no decision rule built on them can separate the classes, and defending against replay requires signals the attacker has not copied, such as challenge-response behaviour or device and network features.

\vspace{-15pt}
\subsubsection{Robustness to Bot-Like Humans}
Legitimate users can also drift towards the bot side of the score, for example with unfamiliar input devices, remote desktops or assistive technology.
As a false-positive check, a fraction $\rho$ of the evaluation humans is replaced by Balabit users \cite{balabit_2016}, recorded over remote desktop, whom the scorer gives a mean $P(\text{bot})$ of 0.45.
Frozen DQN and PPO keep DI at 57.9 and 56.4 at $\rho=1$, above the NP threshold (40.7) and the multi-threshold policy (26.3), because they learned to challenge rather than block. 
The NP multi-threshold does best (59.9), since drifted humans that cross its threshold mostly land in its lower bands, whose challenges humans usually pass (Table~\ref{tab:recal}).
Online training helps only with the label-based reward (PPO 82.3 at $\rho=1$) or audit recalibration (58.1); the score-only reward, which reads the higher scores as evidence of bots, makes it worse (17.5).

\section{DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS}
\subsection{What the Evidence Supports}
With a reliable behavioural score, a learned controller keeps more humans than static thresholds while stopping the same bots: it spends challenges only where the score and the session history point to a bot. 
The score is the component that matters most (DI 16.8 without it).
The Full-RL learners are stable across seeds while contextual bandits are not (10.1-28.3 SD), and the result holds under varying traffic and across reward and simulator constants. 
The scorer itself transfers to a published benchmark once fine-tuned.

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Limitations}
(1) \emph{Deployment reward}: the most robust policies need class information during online training. 
Rewards from observable outcomes alone fail, and scorer-based rewards are only as good as the scorer. 
Labelling a random 2\% of users and recalibrating the score with them recovers most of the label-based performance (Table~\ref{tab:recal}).
When no labels are available, the score-only reward is the fallback, robust to covert bots but not to bot-like humans. 
(2) \emph{Ranking-destroying attacks}: when bots reproduce the features the scorer uses, no policy built on that score can separate them; new signals are needed. 
(3) \emph{Usability is modelled, not measured}: pass rates and times come from published studies of other populations and human abandonment is modelled. 
(4) \emph{Simulator}: no real CAPTCHA provider, no request-rate, CPU or latency model, and no adaptive or VLM-based attacker, although the drift study shows how policies respond when an unseen bot type arrives. 

\vspace{-5pt}
\subsection{Future Work}
A user study of the challenge ladder, abandonment measured at the moment a challenge is shown, integration with real CAPTCHA providers, a queueing model for server load, attackers that adapt to the learned policy, and label-efficient rewards that stay robust when the scorer drifts.

\section{CONCLUSION}
We framed CAPTCHA orchestration as learning a mapping from a calibrated behavioural score and session context to an 11-level intervention ladder, and evaluated it in a simulator grounded in published CAPTCHA user studies, with a leakage-controlled data protocol.
On held-out recordings PPO and DQN reach DI 99.8 and 97.8 against 95.5 for the best static policy, and without the score DQN falls to 16.8. 
% The efficient LSTM scorer, after fine-tuning, classifies every test session of a public web-bot benchmark correctly.
The scorer-only PPO online model adapts to calibration shift due to covert bots, beating all static methods and matching labelled approaches.  
With online training only when dataset drift is detected, score-only PPO matches or exceeds all labelled approaches.
We also shows the limitations of this approach: no reward computed from observable outcomes alone adapted to a ranking-destroying attack, and policies trained or adapted with scorer-derived rewards inherit the scorer's miscalibration.
A practical system therefore needs a calibrated scorer, occasional ground-truth labels and calibration monitoring. 
With these, RL is a viable replacement for hand-tuned score thresholds.
